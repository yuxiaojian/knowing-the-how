Why does the Moon change shape?

The Moon doesn't make its own light, and nothing ever covers part of it. Sunlight always lights exactly half of it. What changes from night to night is how much of that lit half is turned toward us.

    1. Half is always lit

    The Sun lights whichever half of the Moon faces it, just as it does for Earth. Every point in its orbit, the Moon has a day side and a night side.

    2. We see the half facing us

    From Earth we only see the side of the Moon turned toward us (the blue arc). That's a different half from the sunlit one.

    3. The overlap is the phase

    Where the two halves overlap is the part we see lit. Between the Sun and us, the overlap is zero (new moon). Opposite the Sun, it's complete (full moon).

    Wait, doesn't the Moon go around us every day?

    It looks that way, because the Moon rises and sets every day. But that's Earth spinning once every 24 hours and carrying you past the Moon (watch the "You" dot). The Moon itself creeps only about 12° along its orbit each day, so it takes 29.5 days to go all the way around. That slow drift is also why the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day.

      Does it look the same everywhere?

      The phase does. How much of the Moon is lit depends only on where the Sun, Earth and Moon are, so at any moment everyone on Earth sees the same fraction lit. A 60% Moon in San Francisco is a 60% Moon in Tokyo and in Cape Town.

      Which way it's turned doesn't. People in the southern hemisphere stand "upside down" compared to us, so they see the Moon flipped: a waxing Moon is lit on the left. Near the equator the crescent lies on its back like a smile. The angle also shifts a little over the course of a night as the Moon crosses the sky.

      The date can differ too. A full moon at 2 AM in London happens at 6 PM the evening before in San Francisco.

      Why isn't there an eclipse every month?

      In the top-down view it looks like Earth's shadow should hit the Moon at every full moon (a lunar eclipse), and the Moon should block the Sun at every new moon (a solar eclipse).

      It doesn't happen because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° from the plane of Earth's orbit. Most months the full moon passes just above or below Earth's shadow. Eclipses only happen when a full or new moon falls near one of the two places where the orbits cross. That lines up about twice a year, in "eclipse seasons".
